INITIALISING NETWORK MODEL

GRIDLOCK

You are the traffic engineer for a connected city network. Twelve signalised intersections, one budget, one day. Demand rises and incidents happen. There is no setting that removes congestion — only ones that move it somewhere you have to live with.

SELECT OPERATING SCENARIO

Runs entirely offline in the browser. No backend, no network calls, no build step.

OPERATING BRIEF

30 SECOND READ

OBJECTIVE

Keep the whole network moving for one simulated day. You are graded on system-wide throughput, stability, budget discipline, public impact and emergency response — never on clearing a single junction.

READING THE MAP

Roads are coloured by how full they are, from teal through amber to red. Queues at a junction spill back along the road feeding it. A red road three junctions from where you were working is usually your own doing.

CONTROLS

  • Click a junction to inspect and retime its signals.
  • Click a road to reallocate lanes or close the segment.
  • Authorise an emergency corridor when a response vehicle is en route.
  • Scroll to zoom, drag to pan, double-click a junction to focus it.
  • The timeline along the bottom shows what is coming next. NEXT EVENT fast-forwards to just before the next scheduled one instead of waiting it out.
  • Map layers switch the link colouring, place names and the bus network on and off.

KEYS

  • SPACE hold / resume
  • 124 simulation speed
  • ←→ previous / next junction
  • F fit network · ESC clear selection
  • N next scheduled event · R revert last decision

THE PRINCIPLE

Every intervention is a change to a coupled system. Giving one approach more green pushes its platoon into the next junction. Closing a link is not removing traffic — it is relocating it.

When you change something, watch for the small badge that appears on the map about half a minute later. That is the measured consequence, not a scripted message.

SYSTEM INSIGHT

Complex systems are interconnected. An intervention designed to improve one component can alter the behaviour of other components, creating secondary effects. Effective engineering therefore requires evaluating system-wide consequences rather than optimising a single variable.
